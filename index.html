Neural network architecture and hyperparameter effects on automated carbonate thin section classification

Tanya O. Alauddin and Mustafa A. Al Ibrahim

Introduction

Thin section analysis is one of the traditional geologic methods still being utilized today in understanding rocks for hydrocarbon exploration and production workflows. The objective of this paper is to analyze the effect of different neural network architectures and hyperparameters on the identification of Dunham texture of carbonate thin sections. The study assumes the availability of only a small labeled dataset with high-resolution whole slide images.

Methodology

Carbonate intervals from different wells were identified and their thin sections were imaged using a thin section scanner. The thin sections were examined and the dominant Dunham classification (mudstone, wackestone, packstone, grainstone) was assigned as can be seen in Figure 1. Preprocessing the images involved resizing, and cropping the images to remove any pixels related to the glass slide. This was done automatically using neural networks to identify the background. Small patches were extracted from each thin section creating the dataset. Convolutional neural network models are finetuned to the training dataset, and tested on the test dataset to obtain prediction accuracy. Different neural network architectures were tested, for example, ResNet 18 and ResNet 32. In addition, different hyperparameters related to image preprocessing and the neural network were tested, such as image resolution/size and the number of epochs used in training the network.

Results and conclusions

Test results show that the overall accuracy of the test dataset is 94%. The best Dunham class accuracies are 96%, 94%, 91%, and 94%, for mudstones, wackestones, packstones, and grainstones respectively as can be seen in the confusion matrix showing the probability of a correct prediction in Figure 2. When tested on thin sections from a different depth interval, the model was able to predict accurately seven out of eight thin sections. The choice of network architecture had the biggest impact on the accuracy of the results. ResNet 34 overall yielded better accuracy than ResNet 18 if the same hyperparameters are used. The accuracy of the models stayed relatively similar if lower resolution images are used.

Overall, the current model is accurate enough for human-in-the-loop training and practical usage. This will improve its prediction capabilities as more data is injected. In addition, the use of larger network architecture, e.g., ResNet 50 instead of ResNet 34, yields better results with little increase in computational expense. The use of convolutional neural networks and high-resolution thin section images allows for the automation of Dunham classification with high accuracy and consistency, and moving interpretation from qualitative to quantitative. Integration of the machine learning solution as a practical workflow with graphical user interface is essential for adopting the technology. Human-in-the-loop approach provides flexibility for the user and improve confidence in the output.

Deep learning-based grain segmentation and textural analysis from thin section images for reservoir characterization

Tanya O. Alauddin and Mustafa A. Al Ibrahim

Abstract

Petrographic thin section analysis provides insight into grain composition, texture, and pore structure that influence reservoir properties such as porosity, permeability, and fluid flow behavior. However, traditional interpretation relies on manual inspection by experts and is difficult to scale across large datasets. Advances in deep learning and computer vision provide an opportunity to automate petrographic analysis and improve the efficiency of reservoir characterization. In this study, a deep learning–based workflow is developed to automatically identify and segment individual grains in thin section images. Computer vision segmentation models are used to detect grain boundaries and separate grains within the rock matrix. The resulting segmentation outputs enable rapid visualization of grain distributions and support quantitative analysis of rock texture and grain size variability. This automated approach has the potential to accelerate petrographic interpretation and support improved reservoir characterization.

Methods

A deep learning–based workflow was developed to perform instance segmentation of grains in thin section images. The workflow utilizes the Cellpose segmentation model (Stringer et al., 2021), which detects and separates individual objects within microscopy images. Thin section images are processed to identify grain boundaries and segment individual grains within the rock matrix. Each grain is assigned a unique label and visualized through color-coded segmentation maps, enabling rapid identification of grain distributions and spatial relationships within the thin section. These outputs support quantitative analysis of grain size distribution and rock texture. The current implementation was trained and evaluated using a limited dataset of thin section images primarily containing medium-sized grains. The dataset was used to assess the feasibility of applying deep learning–based instance segmentation to petrographic imagery (Al Ibrahim et al., 2023).

Results and discussion

Initial results show that the model can delineate grain boundaries and separate individual grains within the tested thin section images (Figure 1). The segmentation outputs provide clear visualization of grain distributions and allow individual grains to be distinguished within the rock matrix. These results demonstrate the potential of deep learning–based segmentation methods to automate petrographic grain identification and enable quantitative analysis of grain size variability and textural heterogeneity in reservoir rocks. Ongoing work focuses on expanding the training dataset to include a wider range of lithologies, grain sizes, and textures to improve model robustness and generalization. Additional segmentation architectures, including Mask R-CNN (He et al., 2017), will also be evaluated and compared with the current Cellpose-based approach.

Conclusions

Deep learning–based instance segmentation can be applied to petrographic thin section images to automate grain identification and segmentation. Such approaches can accelerate petrographic analysis and enable scalable characterization of rock textures. Automated grain segmentation workflows may support improved reservoir characterization by enabling rapid, quantitative analysis of rock textures that influence key reservoir properties such as porosity and permeability.

Graph-based approaches for permeability prediction and analysis of digital rocks

Tanya O. Alauddin and Rayan Kanfar

Abstract

Digital rock analysis provides a powerful framework for estimating physical and transport properties. Permeability prediction requires computationally expensive fluid-flow simulations, motivating the use of graph-based surrogate models that approximate pore-network connectivity. This study explores graph-based approaches for permeability prediction in complex carbonate digital rocks. Graph-based metrics are also investigated as interpretable descriptors of digital-rock structure and permeability, with potential application in validating synthetically generated rocks against natural samples.

Introduction

Carbonate reservoirs hold much of the world’s hydrocarbon and CO2-storage capacity, but their complex pore structure makes permeability hard to predict from images. Graphs encode the connectivity that controls flow, offering a fast, interpretable surrogate for costly simulation (Liu et al., 2025). In this work, we explore graphs as reduced order models to estimate permeability and analyze digital rocks.

Methods

Segmented micro-CT subvolumes of two carbonates, Ketton (a well-connected oolitic limestone) and Estaillades (microporous), spanning 0.07 to 255 mD, were converted to graphs two ways: an explicit pore network via the SNOW algorithm in PoreSpy and OpenPNM (Gostick, 2017; Gostick et al., 2016), and a topological graph via the Mapper algorithm (Singh et al., 2007; Chung et al., 2024). Geometric and topological metrics were computed with NetworkX, and permeability ground truth is simulated using Stokes-flow from OpenPNM. A random-forest regression model was used to predict permeability and evaluated using cross-validation across the same set of subvolumes.

Results and discussion

Pore-network metrics predicted log-permeability with R2 of about 0.39 (RMSE 0.65; 65 percent within a factor of three) as shown in Figure A. Mapper metrics were far less predictive; a distance-transform (pore-radius) filter improved them from R2 near -0.9 to near 0 but did not match the pore network, indicating that explicit pore-throat connectivity carries permeability while the topological graph mainly reflects porosity. Geometric and topological graph metrics are computed and show correlation with permeability as shown in Figure B.

Conclusions

The SNOW algorithm gives interpretable, modest permeability prediction on two carbonates and outperforms the topological approach, which remains a lightweight, porosity-sensitive complement. Future work will enlarge the dataset, refine topological descriptors, and use these metrics to validate augmented digital rocks.

From geochemical fronts to hydraulic compartments: application of machine learning, geostatistics and segmentation algorithms with physics-based validation

Abul Khair Fahimuddin, Ibrahim Atwah, Waleed Saeed and Tanya Alauddin

Summary

Spatial variability in shallow aquifer salinity provides critical insight into groundwater flow architecture, mixing processes, and subsurface compartmentalization. Despite its importance, regional salinity mapping is often limited by sparse data coverage and reliance on purely interpolative methods. This study presents a hybrid geochemical modeling framework to reconstruct high-resolution salinity distributions and delineate chemically defined compartments within the Smackover aquifer using publicly available USGS geochemical data. Total dissolved solids (TDS) are predicted on a regular grid using a hybrid approach that integrates Universal Kriging with external drift and an XGBoost regression model. This methodology captures both spatial continuity and non-linear geochemical controls that are not adequately represented by conventional geostatistical techniques alone. The resulting salinity surface is subsequently processed using a gradient-based segmentation algorithm to identify sharp chemical fronts, interpreted as boundaries between hydro-chemical domains. Transition zones surrounding these fronts are explicitly buffered to exclude ambiguous mixing regions. The remaining low-gradient interiors are then classified as discrete salinity compartments. To assess physical consistency, the geochemically derived compartments are evaluated through hydraulic flow testing using a simplified groundwater drawdown simulation model. A “safe footprint” analysis determines whether pumping within low-gradient compartment interiors results in limited drawdown propagation across buffered front zones. Numerical simulations demonstrate that compartments defined solely from geochemical gradients exhibit measurable hydraulic coherence, thereby integrating data-driven salinity mapping with physics-based groundwater behavior.

Introduction

Practical understanding of subsurface compartmentalization is central to de-risking drilling operations, optimizing reservoir management, and managing produced water in the oil and gas industry. Salinity contrasts can serve as proxies for hydraulic connectivity, fault sealing capacity, and cross-formational leakage pathways (Yielding et al., 1997). In carbonate aquifers and deep brine systems, compartmentalization directly influences pressure support efficiency, water breakthrough timing, and the sustainability of brine extraction operations (Abdou and Carnegie, 2011). Misinterpretation of connectivity may lead to suboptimal well placement, unexpected pressure depletion, or costly remediation interventions (Birkholzer et al., 2012). Existing approaches for delineating hydro-chemical domains typically rely on contour interpretation, hydro-chemical facies classification, or multivariate statistical clustering (Güler et al., 2002; Souid and Birkle, 2023). While these methods capture compositional similarity, they do not explicitly enforce spatial structure or quantify the sharpness of boundaries between domains. Recent advances in machine learning have introduced algorithms such as Random Forest, Gradient Boosting and deep learning for predictive hydro-chemical mapping (Al-Kindi et al., 2025; Zuo and Xiong 2019). These models can capture non-linear dependencies and interactions among geochemical variables ; however, most applications emphasize prediction accuracy rather than structural interpretation. Hybrid modeling frameworks that combine machine learning with geo-statistics have demonstrated improved predictive performance in environmental and mineral exploration contexts (Abdelmalek and Burton 2024; Humphrey and Cave 2023). However, their application to systematic extraction of salinity-defined compartments in sedimentary aquifers remains limited. The present study addresses these limitations by integrating hybrid geochemical prediction, gradient-based spatial segmentation, and subsequent physics-based validation. In the following sections, salinity is reconstructed using a weighted combination of Universal Kriging with external drift and a machine-learning regression model based on well measurements of elemental concentrations. Hydraulic compartments are then defined objectively using salinity gradient magnitude, ensuring that boundaries reflect spatial contrasts rather than arbitrary concentration thresholds. To assess whether these chemically defined boundaries correspond to meaningful hydraulic isolation, each compartment is evaluated under controlled pumping scenarios by MODFLOW groundwater flow simulation models (Zheng and Wang, 1999).

Theory and Methodology

Let 𝐱=(x,y)\mathbf{x} = (x,y) denote spatial location within an aquifer domain, Ω\Omega and S(𝐱)S\left( \mathbf{x} \right) be the total dissolved solids (TDS) as measure of salinity in water samples. The objective is to reconstruct a continuous salinity field from discrete well measurements of major ions including Na+{Na}^{+}, Ca2+{Ca}^{2 +}, K+K^{+}, Mg2+{Mg}^{2 +}, Ba2+{Ba}^{2 +}, Sr2+{Sr}^{2 +}, Cl−{Cl}^{-}, HCO3−{HCO}_{3}^{-}and SO42−{SO}_{4}^{2 -}. A spatially distributed salinity field, Ŝ(𝐱)\widehat{S}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)\ can be estimated as follows:

Ŝ(𝐱)=αŜUK(𝐱)+(1−α)ŜML(𝐱)(1)\widehat{S}\left( \mathbf{x} \right) = \alpha\,{\widehat{S}}_{UK}\left( \mathbf{x} \right) + (1 - \alpha)\,{\widehat{S}}_{ML}\left( \mathbf{x} \right)\qquad (1)

where ŜUK{\widehat{S}}_{UK} enforces spatial continuity via variogram-based covariance structure (Universal kriging), ŜML{\widehat{S}}_{ML} captures nonlinear geochemical dependencies (XGBoost algorithm), and α∈[0,1]\alpha \in \lbrack 0,1\rbrack is a blending weight determined from cross-validation (Fahimuddin et al., 2025). This formulation preserves basin-scale smoothness while honoring multivariate geochemical controls (Cressie 1993). Based on this high-resolution TDS surface, spatial gradients of the reconstructed salinity field are then computed to quantify chemical contrasts, and simultaneously high-gradient zones are interpreted as hydro-chemical fronts (Zhang and Manheim, 2005). In this case, chemical discontinuities are inferred from the magnitude of the reconstructed field’s spatial gradient, ∇Ŝ(𝐱)\nabla\widehat{S}\left( \mathbf{x} \right):

G(𝐱)=∥∇Ŝ(𝐱)∥=(∂Ŝ∂x)2+(∂Ŝ∂y)2(2)G\left( \mathbf{x} \right) = \left\lVert \nabla\widehat{S}\left( \mathbf{x} \right) \right\rVert = \sqrt{\left( \frac{\partial\widehat{S}}{\partial x} \right)^{2} + \left( \frac{\partial\widehat{S}}{\partial y} \right)^{2}}\qquad (2)

Here the total spatial rate of change of salinity for a given location is denoted as G(𝐱)G\left( \mathbf{x} \right) where higher values indicate sharp salinity contrasts, interpreted as hydro-chemical fronts separating distinct flow or mixing domains. Unlike absolute concentration thresholds, this derivative-based measure identifies structural discontinuities independent of background salinity level (Gillespie et al., 2022). To extract these interfaces and preserve their structural significance, a percentile-based threshold GthrG_{thr} (e.g., upper 20–30% of gradient magnitudes) is applied to G(𝐱)G\left( \mathbf{x} \right). This ensures isolating statistically significant contrasts relative to basin-wide variability (Marr and Hildreth, 1980). Because natural mixing and interpolation uncertainty may produce narrow transitional halos around sharp gradients, a spatial buffering operation is applied to all high-gradient grid cells. This “buffered zone” represents chemically transitional regions where salinity changes rapidly and hydraulic connectivity may be ambiguous. The complement of these buffered fronts defines the operationally stable interior domain:

Ωsafe={𝐱∈Ω|G(𝐱)<Gthr and outside buffer}\Omega_{safe} = \left\{ \mathbf{x} \in \Omega\mspace{6mu} \middle| \mspace{6mu} G\left( \mathbf{x} \right) < G_{thr}\text{ and outside buffer} \right\}

where Ωsafe\Omega_{safe}\ corresponds to spatially smooth, low-gradient regions interpreted as hydro-chemically coherent compartments (Chihi et al., 2015). These compartments exhibit minimal lateral salinity variability and are therefore, considered as structurally consistent units for subsequent hydraulic testing. In this case, each connected region within Ωsafe\Omega_{safe} is labeled as a discrete salinity compartment and forms the basis for physics-based validation under imposed water pumping scenarios. To ensure internal consistency between chemically derived fronts and operational constraints, a quantitative quality control (QC) mismatch metric is computed. Specifically, the percentage of no-drill pixels located within XX grid cells of a compartment-label boundary is evaluated: high % indicates geometric agreement between gradient-derived fronts and buffered exclusion zones. On the contrary, elevated spill percentages suggest over-buffering or misalignment between chemical segmentation and imposed operational masks.

To evaluate whether geochemically derived compartments represent hydraulically meaningful domains, a controlled pumping scenario is imposed at a location, 𝐱0∈Ωsafe\mathbf{x}_{0} \in \Omega_{safe} , i.e., within the interior of a salinity-defined compartment outside buffered transition zones. Late-time drawdown is approximated using the Cooper–Jacob logarithmic approximation derived from the Theis analytical solution for transient radial flow in confined aquifers (Theis, 1935; Cooper and Jacob, 1946) as shown below:

s(𝐱,t)=Q4πTln(2.25Ttr(𝐱)2S)(3)s\left( \mathbf{x},t \right) = \frac{Q}{4\pi T}\ln\left( \frac{2.25\, T\, t}{r(\mathbf{x})^{2}S} \right)\qquad (3)

where r(𝐱)=(x−x0)2+(y−y0)2r\left( \mathbf{x} \right) = \sqrt{(x - x_{0})^{2} + (y - y_{0})^{2}}, is radial distance from a chosen pumping location, QQ is pumping rate, TT is transmissivity, and SS is storativity. The compartment outline, Ωsafe,\Omega_{safe}, directly governs where hydraulic response is considered valid and where propagation of drawdown across fronts must be evaluated. Hydraulic validation metrics include maximum drawdown within Ωsafe\Omega_{safe} and drawdown intrusion into buffered front zones. If drawdown remains largely confined within a compartment and does not significantly propagate across salinity fronts, the chemically derived segmentation can be considered hydraulically coherent with confidence.

Example

The proposed methodology has been implemented as a representative synthetic use case by leveraging data from the openly available U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) produced water geochemical database. This dataset contains geochemical and associated meta data for more than 100,000 produced water and deep formation water samples over 300 wells (Blondes et al., 2019). In this study, the Smackover formation has been chosen to demonstrate validity of the proposed method using public data, and this should not be considered as a fully calibrated hydraulic model of the Smackover system. For the numerical experiments, the theoretical framework described in the previous section, was implemented through a modular software workflow developed in Python and associated Sci-kit learn libraries (Pedregosa et al., 2011). This application sequentially reconstructs the salinity field, extracts geochemical fronts, delineates compartments, and performs physics-based validation. The geo-spatial interpolation closely follows methodology described in Fahimuddin et al. 2025, and related references. Figure 1 presents the hybrid reconstruction of total dissolved solids (TDS) across the Smackover Formation, generated using a weighted combination of Universal Kriging and XGBoost regression. A pronounced northwest–southeast salinity gradient is observed, with TDS increasing from approximately 120,000 mg/L to values exceeding 350,000 mg/L. Contour lines display coherent, elongated trends aligned with regional structural orientation of the Smackover trend. The absence of artificial “bulls-eye’ artifacts bolstering the validity of the regional geo-spatial predictability of TDS trend, based on this hybrid approach.

Figure 2 illustrates the sequential transformation of the reconstructed Smackover TDS surface into hydraulically testable salinity compartments. Starting from the hybrid TDS map, the spatial gradient magnitude is computed to quantify the rate of lateral salinity change (Figure 2a). High |∇S| values delineate narrow, elongated zones where salinity varies sharply over short distances, reflecting potential hydro-chemical discontinuities. These features trend consistently with the regional NW–SE structural fabric as already observed in Figure 1. In contrast, broad low-gradient interiors indicate chemically homogeneous regions with smooth lateral continuity. To objectively isolate structurally significant discontinuities, the gradient field is normalized using percentile-based scaling to generate a front-likelihood surface ranging from 0 to 1. This transformation suppresses background variability while emphasizing statistically significant contrasts relative to basin-wide variability. High-likelihood zones cluster along the previously identified gradient ridges, providing a probabilistic representation of chemical fronts rather than a binary threshold. By applying a percentile threshold to the front-likelihood field isolates the most significant discontinuities (threshold range derived through sensitivity analysis). The resulting binary mask (Figure 2b) delineates chemically ambiguous boundary corridors where salinity changes rapidly and hydraulic connectivity may be uncertain. These zones represent exclusion regions rather than compartments. The complement of the buffered boundary mask defines a spatially coherent interior domain, Ωsafe\Omega_{safe}, consisting of low-gradient regions with internally smooth salinity structure. Connected-component labeling within this stable interior domain yields discrete salinity compartments (Figure 2c). Each compartment is, therefore, geometrically bounded by statistically significant chemical fronts and exhibits internal salinity continuity which satisfy the definition of a hydro-chemically coherent unit (Chihi et al., 2015). Finally, the operational drilling footprint is derived by excluding all buffered boundary zones from the valid salinity domain. The resulting drillable mask (Figure 2d) represents the spatial subset of Ωsafe\Omega_{safe} where salinity variability is minimal and proximity to fronts, is avoided. Approximately 7.8% of the mapped Smackover domain satisfies the defined safe-footprint criteria. This region forms the basis for subsequent physics-based hydraulic validation under imposed water extraction scenarios.

Figure 3a quantifies the geometric consistency between gradient-derived compartment boundaries and the imposed no-drill buffer. For a buffer distance of X=6 grid cells, only 7.0% of no-drill pixels are located within the prescribed proximity of a compartment boundary, whereas 93.0% fall beyond this threshold (“spill”). In addition, the drillable footprint (Ωsafe\Omega_{safe}) represents 7.8% of the valid aquifer domain. The relatively low QC % indicates that the buffered no-drill zone extends well beyond immediate high-gradient ridges, while the limited safe-area percentage confirms that only a small fraction of the basin meets the defined stability criteria. Figure 3b further evaluates hydraulic behavior of this drillable footprint under a representative pumping scenario of 500 m³/day over five years using MODFLOW simulation. The maximum predicted drawdown across the modeled domain is 0.41 m, while maximum drawdown within no-drill regions, is limited to 0.10 m, indicating substantial hydraulic attenuation across chemically defined boundaries.

Conclusions

This study presents a structured workflow for delineating salinity compartments by integrating hybrid geostatistical and machine learning reconstruction with gradient-based front detection and physics-informed hydraulic validation. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the reconstructed salinity field remains dependent on data density and potential spatial sampling bias. Second, percentile thresholds and buffer distances are tunable parameters and may introduce subjectivity into data-driven boundary delineation. Third, the current implementation is primarily two-dimensional and does not explicitly resolve vertical stratification or multi-layer connectivity. Despite these limitations, the methodology quantitatively integrates geo-chemical segmentation with simulated groundwater response. The proposed workflow therefore bridges the gap between descriptive hydrochemistry and predictive reservoir-scale decision-making.
